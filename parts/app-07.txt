    <div class="card">
      <div class="h3">Why do CNNs use filters on small regions at a time?</div>
      <p class="small">The main reason is that many useful visual features are <strong>local</strong>. An edge, corner, short stroke, or small texture pattern depends mostly on nearby pixels, not on the entire image at once.</p>

      <div class="card center math-cell">\[\text{pixels}\longrightarrow\text{local edges/strokes}\longrightarrow\text{larger shapes}\longrightarrow\text{object}\]</div>

      <p class="small">For example, when recognizing a handwritten digit, the network does not need to understand the whole digit immediately. It can first detect short local strokes and edges, then later layers combine those simpler features into larger structures.</p>

      <div class="grid cards3 small">
        <div class="card">
          <strong>1. Local features are meaningful</strong>
          <p>Nearby pixels can form edges, corners, curves, and short strokes that are useful building blocks.</p>
        </div>
        <div class="card">
          <strong>2. The same feature can appear anywhere</strong>
          <p>The same small filter can slide across the image and search for the same pattern at many different locations.</p>
        </div>
        <div class="card">
          <strong>3. Fewer parameters</strong>
          <p>A small filter needs far fewer weights than a filter that spans the entire image.</p>
        </div>
      </div>

      <p class="small">If we used one large filter on the entire image, that filter would be tied much more strongly to one global arrangement of pixels and would require many more parameters. A small filter is more flexible because it can be reused everywhere.</p>

      <div class="card panel small">
        <strong>Key idea:</strong> a CNN starts by asking small local questions such as “Is there an edge here?” or “Is there a short stroke here?” Later layers combine the answers to those local questions into more complicated features.
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="card">
      <div class="h3">Step 2 · A convolution filter looks at a small local region</div>
      <p class="small">A filter is a small matrix of weights. A common size is \(3\times 3\).</p>
      <div class="grid cards2 small">
        <div class="card center">
          <div class="muted">A \(3\times3\) image patch</div>
          <div style="line-height:1.9">
            \(p_1\;\;p_2\;\;p_3\)<br>
            \(p_4\;\;p_5\;\;p_6\)<br>
            \(p_7\;\;p_8\;\;p_9\)
          </div>
        </div>
        <div class="card center">
          <div class="muted">A \(3\times3\) filter</div>
          <div style="line-height:1.9">
            \(w_1\;\;w_2\;\;w_3\)<br>
            \(w_4\;\;w_5\;\;w_6\)<br>
            \(w_7\;\;w_8\;\;w_9\)
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>
      <p class="small">At one location, the filter computes a weighted sum, just like the neuron in Section 1:</p>
      <div class="card center math-cell">\[z=w_1p_1+w_2p_2+\cdots+w_9p_9+b\]</div>
      <p class="small">The filter then moves to a nearby part of the image and repeats the same calculation using the <strong>same weights</strong>.</p>
